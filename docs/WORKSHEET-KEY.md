# Salem Village, 1692 — Answer Key

**Do not hand this out.** Point totals match `WORKSHEET.md` and add to 100, plus 4 extra credit.

**How to grade this quickly.** Most items are one line. Anything in *italics* below is the standard of "credit," not a required wording — students answer in their own words and will phrase these very differently. Where a question has a checkable fact (a date, a number, a name), that fact is **bolded**; take it or don't.

**The one thing worth being strict about:** questions asking *who told you* are the point of the whole assignment. A student who writes a correct fact with no source has half the answer, because the game's entire argument is that where a claim came from is part of the claim.

## Part 1 — The Memorial · 6 pts

**1.** *(2)* The carved quotations are real things the accused said at their trials, and **every one runs into the base of the wall and stops mid-sentence**. It is not damage — the memorial was designed that way, to say that these people were cut off. *1 pt for noticing they stop mid-sentence, 1 pt for recognising it as deliberate.*

**2.** *(3, 1 each)*
- **First panel** — what happened: the arrests, the deaths, the timeline. **More than 150 arrested; 19 hanged, 1 pressed to death, at least 5 died in jail**, all inside one year.
- **Second panel** — how the court worked: spectral evidence, no lawyers, and confession as the way to survive.
- **Third panel** — why: the four things historians still argue about. This is the only place the game asks its question out loud.

**3.** *(1)* It says *"You will want to remember this one."* Accept any reasonable reading: the game is telling you in advance that this name will matter, before you have any reason to care — which is the whole seven-chapter structure. *Full credit for engaging with why it is said early.*

## Part 2 — March 1692 · 24 pts

**4.** *(6, 1 each)* Many correct answers per row; credit any accurate main idea.
- **Mercy Lewis** — the village is at war over its minister; the witch cake and the girls beginning to name names; she is from Maine and her parents were killed there.
- **Rev. Parris** — his salary and firewood were agreed in writing and the village has stopped paying; the committee elected in October is made of men who opposed him.
- **Tituba** — she is enslaved in the household, carries the wood, and was told by Mary Sibley to bake the cake. She did as she was told.
- **Ingersoll** — keeps the tavern and hears everything; the girls, Dr. Griggs finding nothing, the witch cake; the village is split Putnam against Porter.
- **Ann Putnam Jr.** — twelve; her family's grievances, especially the Topsfield boundary line her father argues about.
- **Rebecca Nurse** — 71, holds land in her own name; the seating; the quarrel between her family and the Putnams is old and has never come to anything.

**5.** *(4, 1 each)*
- **Woodpile** — nearly empty in the first week of March. The village agreed to supply the minister's firewood and stopped.
- **Seating chart** — every household is assigned a seat by wealth and status, nailed up where the whole village reads it twice a week.
- **Account book** — who owes whom. Debt as a map of who cannot afford to disagree.
- **Boundary stone** — a disputed line, with the notch **recut at least once**. Somebody walked out into the woods to argue about it again.

**6.** *(2)* **Putnam.** *1 pt for the name.* *1 pt for the inference:* a family with money out to half the households on the page is a family it is expensive to contradict.

**7.** *(8, 2 each)* 1 pt for the date and label, 1 pt for the main idea.

| Paper | Date | Label | Main idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| Village agreement with Mr. Parris | **1689** | **RECONSTRUCTED** | The promise, in writing, that the village then broke |
| The seating list | **1690** | **RECONSTRUCTED** | Status, priced, and posted publicly |
| Ingersoll's account page | **1691–92** | **RECONSTRUCTED** | Debt as a map of who cannot afford to disagree |
| The Topsfield petition | **1680s** | **RECONSTRUCTED** | A land quarrel thirty years old, same families on each side |

**8.** *(2)* The two things are **the witch cake** (rye meal and the girls' urine, baked and fed to a dog, to make the witch cry out) and **the girls beginning to name names**, which happened within about a week of it. She is asking which caused the other — and pointing out that the village has already decided.

**9.** *(2)* **Credit any honest answer.** This is a first impression captured before it can be revised; there is no right answer and the sheet says so. Zero only for a blank.

## Part 3 — The parsonage dig · 4 pts

**10.** *(2)* **Two rooms and a loft, one chimney. Nine or ten people** — Parris, his wife, three children, Tituba and John Indian.

**11.** *(2)* There was nowhere in that house for a child to be ill unobserved. Accept anything reaching that: fits in a two-room house are witnessed by everybody, immediately, all the time.

## Part 4 — June 1692 · 22 pts

**12.** *(2)* She has been arrested and is in the jail at Salem town. **They came for her on the 24th of March**, ill in bed. Told by **Francis Nurse**, her husband, in the dooryard. *1 pt for what happened, 1 pt for naming Francis.*

**13.** *(5, 1 each)*
- **Francis Nurse** — she is in the Salem jail; he has written to the court and carried a petition round the village.
- **Rebecca Nurse** — cold, in irons some days, charged for every day she is held; calm, and certain it will be sorted out.
- **Tituba** — she confessed in March and is still in the cellar in June. There is a four-year-old down there.
- **Mary Warren** — she said in April that none of it was true, was jailed herself, and took it back. She no longer knows what she believes.
- **Ann Putnam Jr.** — her father writes her depositions; she says them, he sets them down, and it is read back to her.

**14.** *(8, 2 each)*

| Paper | Date | Label | Main idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| Petition for Rebecca Nurse | **1692** | **CLOSE** | The whole village saying she is not a witch — and it not mattering |
| Warrant for Rebecca Nurse | **23 March 1692** | **CLOSE** | The machinery starting; a printed-form kind of document |
| Ann Putnam's deposition | **1692** | **RECONSTRUCTED** | Spectral evidence sworn by a twelve-year-old — look at the handwriting |
| The jail keeper's bill | **1692** | **RECONSTRUCTED** | They were charged for their own imprisonment, including the irons |

**15.** *(2)* **Thirty-nine** neighbours signed, including **Putnams**. *1 pt.* It made no difference — she was tried and hanged anyway. *1 pt.*

**16.** *(2)* **Her father, Thomas Putnam**, writes them. *1 pt.* The document is therefore evidence of what her father wrote down and read back to her, not a direct record of a twelve-year-old's words — and he had a long memory for what had been done to his family. *1 pt.*

**17.** *(3)* **Credit any honest answer, including "it has not changed."** Full marks require a reason. This question and Q9 together are the most interesting thing on the sheet to read.

## Part 5 — The archive · 10 pts

**18.** *(4, 1 each)*
- **Spectral evidence** — testimony that the accused's *shape* or spirit appeared to a witness. Nobody else could see it and nobody could disprove it. Being demonstrably somewhere else was not a defence; it was the accusation.
- **Lawyers** — there were none. The accused had no defence counsel.
- **Charter** — Massachusetts had lost its royal charter; the colony's legal footing was uncertain and the court trying these cases was itself on shaky ground.
- **Confession** — **nobody who confessed was executed.** Confessing, naming others, and staying alive was the rational move.

**19.** *(3)* Every person hanged was somebody who refused to lie to save their life. The system rewarded false confession and killed honesty. *Full credit for reaching that inversion in any words.*

**20.** *(3)* Any **two** of:
- The symptoms do not match ergotism.
- Whole households ate bread from the same grain, but only certain people were afflicted.
- It says nothing about **who was accused** — it cannot explain the pattern in the names.
- *(Also creditable:* it is a single tidy cause that explains one symptom and none of the pattern.*)*

## Part 6 — September 1692 · 14 pts

**21.** *(6, 2 each)*
- **Giles Corey** — refused to plead, asked three times in the proper form, stood mute all three. Laid under boards with weights added until he should plead. **It took two days. He was 81.**
- **Burial** — they were not buried. Put in the rocks by the ledge where it was done — no ground, no service, no stone, because they were excommunicate. Some families are said to have gone out at night and brought their people home.
- **Is it over** — **eight died on the 22nd**; since then no new warrants he has heard of. People are beginning to say out loud that spectral evidence will not do, and ministers in Boston have written against it.

**22.** *(2)* If a man is tried and convicted the sheriff takes his farm; if he is never tried, the sheriff cannot. By refusing to plead he prevented a trial and kept the land, which went to his sons-in-law. *1 pt.* It cost him two days of being pressed to death. *1 pt.*

**23.** *(6, 2 each)*

| Paper | Date | Label | Main idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Corey record | **19 September 1692** | **RECONSTRUCTED** | Two days, recorded in the same language as a boundary dispute. Follow the money |
| Sheriff's return on the death warrant | **22 September 1692** | **RECONSTRUCTED** | Eight people, one line each — a completed piece of paperwork |
| Mary Easty's petition | **September 1692** | **CLOSE** | She is not asking for her own life; she is asking the court to stop killing others |

## Part 8 — Ranking the causes · 16 pts

**26.** *(12, 3 each row)* **There is no correct ranking and no ranking should be penalised.** Award per row:
- **1 pt** — a case is named and ranked.
- **1 pt** — the evidence given is real and actually in the game.
- **1 pt** — the evidence genuinely supports that case.

Evidence that should earn full credit:

| Case | Strong evidence from the game |
|---|---|
| **A · Land and money** | Ingersoll's account page (Putnam debts); the Topsfield petition; the boundary stone with the recut notch; the seating list; the parsonage woodpile and the salary agreement; Ann Putnam Jr. on her father's grievances |
| **B · The war** | Mercy Lewis on Casco Bay and her parents; the refugee girls living in the accusing households; the watchman on the night watch and the fighting eastward; Dr. Whitfield on the magistrates who had held frontier commands |
| **C · Who was accused** | Tituba on the first three accused — a beggar, a woman who went to law against her own sons, an enslaved woman; Rebecca Nurse on holding land in her own name; Dr. Whitfield on Karlsen and inheritance |
| **D · The law itself** | Spectral evidence; no counsel; the lost charter; nobody who confessed was executed; the jail bill; Giles Corey and forfeiture; Ann Putnam's deposition written by her father |

*Do not give credit for evidence that is not in the game (e.g. "mass hysteria", "teenage girls lying", "the Crucible").*

**27.** *(4)* Full credit requires a **real** limitation of the case they ranked first, not a general "it's complicated." Good answers, by case:
- **A (land and money)** — does not explain Tituba, who owned nothing and was owned; does not explain why it took the specific form of witchcraft rather than a lawsuit; does not explain accusations spreading to Andover and Beverly.
- **B (the war)** — does not explain the first three accused, none of whom had anything to do with Maine; does not explain Giles Corey or George Burroughs.
- **C (who was accused)** — does not explain Giles Corey, a man of 81, or George Burroughs, a minister; does not explain the timing, since these women were in the same position for years beforehand.
- **D (the law itself)** — explains how it was possible and how it killed so many, but not why it started here, in this village, in this year, with these names.

*The model answer for full marks is Dr. Whitfield's own move: the case explains a great deal, and then stops.*

## Extra credit · 4 pts

**E1.** *(2)* Any genuine contradiction from the game. The six built in are: what happened in the parsonage before Tituba was examined; whether the rye was to blame; who actually swore out the complaints; how names arrive in a room; what started it; and Ingersoll's own part in it. *1 pt for a real pair with both sides stated, 1 pt for a reasoned preference — including "I cannot tell," which the game treats as a legitimate finding and so should you.*

**E2.** *(2)* Because the difference between a transcription and a reconstruction is the difference between evidence and a plausible-sounding rendering — and a student who cannot tell those apart cannot evaluate a source. Presenting invented text as a quotation from a primary source would teach exactly the wrong habit in a game about what the record does and does not say.
