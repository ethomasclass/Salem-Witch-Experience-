# Salem 1692: a thirty-minute walk through a village coming apart

> **Design handoff — working title needed**

A top-down exploration game for high school US History. You walk a small Puritan
village three times across one year, talk to the people living in it, and travel
forward occasionally to ask historians what you just saw.

| **Audience** | High school |
| **Runtime** | ~30 min |

## The premise — you watch it happen to people you have met

The player is a student from the present who can visit Salem Village in 1692.
They cannot change anything. They walk around, talk to people, notice things,
and go home.

The question the game asks — *what caused this?* — is never posed as an essay
prompt. It arrives because the player spent time in a functioning village in
March, came back in June to find two people they'd talked to in jail, and came
back in September to find houses standing empty. By then they want to know why,
and they have opinions.

Learning happens through **people and place**, not through documents. This is a
game about a society, and the causes of the trials are things you absorb by
living in it for half an hour — not things you read on a worksheet.

---

## Structure — one village, three passes, four trips home

Do not spread thirty minutes across a large map. Build one small village and
dress it three times. It's cheap to produce and it's the entire emotional engine
of the game.

Total runtime ≈ 32 min.

### Present · ≈ 3 min — Chapter zero: the memorial

**The game opens in the present day, at the Salem Witch Trials Memorial on
Charter Street.** Not a generic "present" — this location specifically, because
it is also the closing location. One map, built once, used twice, and the game
becomes a loop instead of a line.

Three things happen here, and none of them is a text crawl:

1. **The threshold stones.** The entrance is paved with the victims' own
   protests of innocence, and every inscription runs into the base of the wall
   and stops mid-sentence. That is a real feature of the 1992 memorial, and it
   is the entire thesis of the game in one object the player physically walks
   over. It fires on being stepped on, not on being examined — too important to
   leave to chance.
2. **Twenty benches**, each cut with a name, a means of execution, and a date.
   All identical: the memorial refuses to rank the dead or make any one of them
   the interesting one, and so does the game.
3. **One person to talk to** — Nora, seventeen, summer job. Peer voice.

**The player is given a name before they meet anybody.** The bench beside the
gap in the north wall reads REBECCA NURSE · HANGED · JULY 19, 1692. So the
player walks into her kitchen in March already knowing the date she dies, and
knowing it while she does not. When she says *"everyone here has something
between them and somebody, it has never once come to anything"* — that lands
like a stone.

This inverts the usual mystery structure on purpose: **give them the outcome,
withhold the cause.** Knowing whodunnit and investigating *why* is what
historical thinking actually is, and it is what the assignment asks for.

**Do not explain the time travel.** Any mechanism — a device, a dream, an
exhibit — invites a clever sixteen-year-old to interrogate the mechanism instead
of the history. The player steps through a gap in the wall. Nobody remarks on
it, then or ever.

They arrive at the **Salem Town** end of the road, so the first thing they do in
1692 is walk five miles from the money to the village that resents it. The road
walk stops being an optional detour and becomes the opening.

### March 1692 · ≈ 8 min — Nothing has happened yet

Two girls in the parsonage are sick and nobody knows what it means. This chapter
is pure introduction: who's rich, who's bitter, who married whose cousin, who
stopped delivering the minister's firewood. It should play like the sleepy first
town in an RPG. Every grudge the player learns here pays off later.

### Present · ≈ 2 min — The parsonage foundation

An archaeologist standing in the excavated cellar hole. What kind of place was
this, materially? How poor, how cold, how close together did these people live?

### June 1692 · ≈ 8 min — The village fills with strangers

Ingersoll's tavern is packed with people from other towns who came to watch. Two
people the player talked to in March are in jail. Everyone's account of their
neighbors has changed, and some of them are now lying. The petty contradictions
from chapter one are turning lethal.

### Present · ≈ 2 min — Why could nobody defend themselves?

A legal historian on spectral evidence, the absence of defense counsel, and the
colony's lost charter. This is the interlude that reframes the trials from
"people were superstitious" to "the court had rules, and the rules were the
problem."

### Sept 1692 · ≈ 6 min — Empty houses

The worst of it. The families of the hanged were denied burial in the churchyard;
some went out at night to bring the bodies home anyway. People who were friendly
in March now say nothing at all. No locked doors, no gates — just silence where a
conversation used to be.

**The noticing.** Once, late in this chapter, one person looks directly at the
player and speaks to them — Ann Putnam Jr. is the candidate. The player has been
invisible for two chapters and is still unable to act; that is the point. The
helplessness stops being a UI constraint and becomes the payload. Build exactly
one of these. A second would make it a mechanic instead of a rupture.

### Present · ≈ 3 min — The memorial

A descendant with ancestors on both sides of it — one hanged, one who signed the
complaint. Thousands of living people are in exactly this position. The game ends
by asking the player, once, what they think caused it. The answer is not graded.

## Core principle — the primary sources are people, not paper

A deposition *is* someone's testimony. So don't hand the player a document to
read — let the person say it aloud, in their own voice, standing in their own
kitchen. The player gets authentic period content without ever being assigned
reading.

Reserve physical documents for the two or three things that only work as objects:
something with a signature on it, something with a number in it, something
someone did not mean for the player to see. If a screen in this game looks like a
graphic organizer, cut it.

---

## Clues — the causes are in the furniture

What the player finds should be environmental and concrete, not textual. These
are the ones worth building:

- **The meetinghouse seating chart** — Pews assigned by wealth and standing — a
  literal map of who matters, nailed to the wall, seen by every person in the
  village every Sunday. A player who notices this understands the faction fight
  without a word of explanation.
- **The parsonage woodpile** — Reverend Parris's contract promised him firewood.
  The village stopped delivering it. The minister is cold, and he is preaching
  about enemies inside his own congregation.
- **The road to Salem Town** — Five miles, and visibly richer at the far end.
  Make the player walk it once and the village-versus-town resentment becomes
  something they have in their legs.
- **A boundary marker in the woods** — Between the Nurse and Putnam land. Small,
  easy to miss, and the whole faction thesis hangs off it.
- **The account book at Ingersoll's** — Who owes whom. Debt as a map of
  resentment.

---

## Conversation — talking needs friction or it's a lecture with walking

- **NPCs know who you've been talking to.** "Who told you that? Was it Putnam?" —
  and their story shifts based on the answer.
- **Gossip is currency.** A village of six hundred people where everyone's
  business is public. Carry a rumor from one house to another and a door opens.
- **People contradict each other,** and the game never adjudicates. In March the
  contradictions are petty. By September they are getting people killed.
- **Some people stop talking to you.** Not a locked door — someone who was
  friendly in March and now has nothing to say. This lands harder than any gate.
- **Some things you can't ask about yet.** Gating by knowledge, not by items: you
  can't ask Putnam about the Topsfield line until you've stood at the boundary
  marker.

---

## Causation — it was a conspiracy, not a lone killer

Four causal threads, all real, all partially true, none sufficient alone. The
player should leave able to argue a weighting between them — that *is* the
historical thinking skill.

- **Land, faction, and money** — Salem Village against Salem Town; Putnam against
  Porter; the geography of who accused whom. (Boyer & Nissenbaum, *Salem
  Possessed*.)
- **War trauma on the frontier** — King William's War, the Maine raids, and
  refugees living in the households doing the accusing. (Norton, *In the Devil's
  Snare*.)
- **Gender, reputation, and inheritance** — Who the accused women were, and what
  they stood to inherit. (Karlsen, *The Devil in the Shape of a Woman*.)
- **Law and cosmology** — Spectral evidence admitted in court, no defense
  counsel, a colony that had lost its charter, and a theology in which confession
  was survival.

> **The red herring — build this deliberately**
>
> **Ergot poisoning.** Let the player find it and feel clever: it's famous, it's
> tidy, it explains the hallucinations. Then let them find the rebuttal — the
> symptoms don't match, whole households ate the same bread, and it says nothing
> about *who* got accused. Teaching that the satisfying single-cause story is
> usually the wrong one may be the most valuable ninety seconds in the game.

## Cast · 1692 — ten people worth building

Real people, using their documented words where possible. Invent only connective
tissue.

| Person | What they carry |
|---|---|
| **Ann Putnam Jr.** · 12 | Filed more formal complaints than almost anyone. In 1706 stood in the village church while her apology was read aloud. Meeting her at twelve and learning about that apology at the end is the spine of the game. |
| **Mercy Lewis** · ~19 | A servant in the Putnam house, orphaned when her parents were killed in the Maine frontier raids. She is the war-trauma thesis standing in a kitchen. |
| **Tituba** | Enslaved in the Parris household, most likely Indigenous rather than Black — a detail later retellings got wrong, which is itself worth teaching. Her confession under a beating supplied the imagery everyone else then borrowed. She confessed, and she lived. |
| **Rebecca Nurse** · 71 | Devout, propertied, a full church member, and thirty-nine neighbors signed a petition for her. The jury acquitted; the room erupted; they were asked to reconsider; they convicted. Proof this was never only about outcasts. |
| **Sarah Good, and Dorothy Good** · 4 | The other pole — homeless, pregnant, begging. Her four-year-old was jailed in irons and testified against her own mother. The Nurse/Good contrast is the sharpest single piece of evidence in the game. |
| **Giles Corey** · 81 | Pressed to death for refusing to enter a plea. Refusing to plead kept his estate from forfeiture. Follow the money. |
| **George Burroughs** | Former village minister, survived the Maine raids, hanged in August. Recited the Lord's Prayer perfectly on the ladder — supposedly impossible for a witch — and the crowd nearly stopped it. Minister, war, and faction in one man. |
| **Mary Warren** | Afflicted, then recanted, then was accused herself, then rejoined the afflicted. Demonstrates the machine's logic better than any argument: the only exit from accusation is accusation. |
| **Rev. Samuel Parris** | Salary disputes, firewood disputes, and sermons about enemies within the congregation. The faction engine. |
| **Sewall & Stoughton** | Use them as a pair. Judge Samuel Sewall stood in church in 1697 and publicly took the blame. Chief Justice William Stoughton, who admitted spectral evidence in the first place, never apologized and became governor. |

### On the bench, if budget allows

Thomas Brattle, whose October 1692 letter dismantles the court's reasoning —
valuable because it forecloses "they didn't know any better." Cotton Mather and
Robert Calef as a deliberately contradictory pair. Mary Easty, whose final
petition asks not for her own life but that no one else die.

## Cast · present — fictional composites, real positions

Make the modern experts **fictional**. Writing invented dialogue for a named
living historian puts words in a real person's mouth. Their positions should be
the real historiographical schools, with the actual books credited on screen so a
curious student can chase them.

> **The rule for every present-day character: they ask questions, they don't
> answer them.**
>
> The moment a modern character starts explaining the seventeenth century, the
> game turns into a worksheet with a walking animation. Nora doesn't tell the
> player what to think about the gift shops — she says her cousin works at one
> and it pays better than her job does, and that nobody in 1692 called
> themselves a witch, and that she doesn't have a solution, she just notices it.
>
> The single exception is the legal historian on spectral evidence. That
> genuinely is technical, and it is the interlude that reframes the trials from
> "people were superstitious" to "the court had rules, and the rules were the
> problem."

- **The archaeologist** at the parsonage foundation — what objects say that
  people don't.
- **The legal historian** — spectral evidence, the lost charter, no defense
  counsel.
- **The descendant** — ancestors on both sides of the memorial.
- **The shop owner in Witch City** — witch kitsch, Halloween tourism, twenty
  deaths as a brand. Not a villain. A genuinely uncomfortable question about who
  profits from this history.
- **The local teenager** working the memorial — peer voice, and the natural home
  for the story of the eighth-graders whose petition got Elizabeth Johnson Jr.
  exonerated in 2022.

> **Gap to close before writing the frontier thread**
>
> The war-trauma material is told entirely as settler trauma unless a Wabanaki
> voice is in it. Work from published interviews or actual consultation rather
> than writing that character from scratch.

## Constraints — content and craft rules

- **The player is an observer and cannot act** — No mechanic in which the player
  accuses anyone, even as a "choice." These were real people who were killed. It
  also dodges every time-travel paradox a clever sixteen-year-old will raise. The
  September noticing does not weaken this: being seen is not being able to help.
- **Violence is carried by testimony, never depicted** — Giles Corey's pressing,
  Dorothy Good in irons, the beating that produced Tituba's confession: the player
  learns each of these because a person tells them, flatly, standing in a kitchen.
  Nothing violent renders on screen. This is not squeamishness — the surviving
  records *are* testimony, so hearsay-at-one-remove is the historically honest
  form. It also happens to be the version that survives a parent email.
- **Two palettes, one map** — 1692 in cold greys and muted earth; the present
  warmer and more saturated. Same geography in both eras so the locations rhyme —
  meetinghouse to memorial, parsonage to dig site.
- **Two screens Pokémon never had** — If any documents survive into the build,
  they need a real full-screen reader with original text and a modernized gloss
  side by side. And conversations need close-up portraits — a four-line dialogue
  box can't show someone reacting.
- **Browser, no accounts, no data collection** — School Chromebooks on filtered
  networks. localStorage only, for COPPA/FERPA reasons. Plain HTML5 canvas or
  Phaser 3 over a Godot web export — the wasm payload is rough on school hardware
  and this game is 95% dialogue.
- **An exit artifact for the teacher** — The player's final answer, exportable as
  plain text they can paste into an assignment. Without it this is a fun thirty
  minutes with no assessment hook. Scoped to **general US History**: a plain
  claim-plus-evidence paragraph that rewards sourcing and causal weighting, not
  AP rubric point-counting. If a DBQ is wanted, it happens afterward on paper —
  the game is the research phase.
- **One pass, four threads in parallel** — All four causal threads are reachable
  in a single ~30 min run. Fits one class period, and every student arrives at
  discussion having seen the same evidence base — so they differ on *weighting*,
  which is the argument worth having. No thread may be gated behind a replay.

## Two registers

The game runs in two voices and the gap between them is doing work.

**1692** is period-inflected but readable. No "forsooth", no dialect spelling.
Where a period word carries real meaning it is used *and glossed in dialogue* —
Ingersoll says "I keep the ordinary — that's what we call a tavern"; Parris
explains that the rate is the tax that pays his salary. The UI always uses the
plain modern word.

**The present** is plain contemporary English, with contractions, hesitations
and jokes. Nora sounds seventeen because she is. If a present-day scene could be
mistaken for a museum placard, rewrite it.

Within 1692, register also tracks character: Ann Putnam Jr. (12) and Mercy
Lewis (19) use contractions; Parris, Rebecca Nurse and Ingersoll speak more
formally. That is characterisation as well as readability.

## Decisions

0. **Open in the present, at the memorial.** ~3 min chapter zero. See the
   structure section. Total runtime moves to ~32 min; March is not cut.
1. **Player stake — someone notices you.** Pure observer through March and June.
   One rupture in September, and only one. See the Sept 1692 beat.
2. **Course — general US History.** The ending prioritizes sourcing and causal
   weighting over AP rubric points. Exit artifact is a claim-plus-evidence
   paragraph.
3. **Intensity — aftermath and testimony.** Nothing violent depicted on screen;
   every atrocity arrives as something a person tells you.
4. **Structure — single pass, threads parallel.** No causal thread gated behind
   a replay.
