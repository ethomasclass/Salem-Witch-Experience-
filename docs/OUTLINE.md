# Salem Village, 1692 — outline for the rest of the game

Chapter zero (the memorial) and chapter one (March) are built. This is the
plan for everything after them, plus the document-collection mechanic that
runs through all of it.

Nothing here is written as dialogue yet. This is the shape.

**Read [`DESIGN.md`](DESIGN.md) first** — the rules it sets are assumed
throughout, in particular: people not paper, the player cannot act, violence
arrives as testimony, and no screen may look like a graphic organiser.

---

## The document mechanic — you copy, you don't take

### Why not a normal inventory

Two design-doc rules make a conventional collectible system wrong here:

> *Gating by knowledge, not by items.*
>
> *If a screen in this game looks like a graphic organizer, cut it.*

A satchel of thirty pickups turns a game about weighing testimony into a
scavenger hunt, and students optimise for the counter instead of the
argument. But the same doc reserves exactly the space this needs:

> *Reserve physical documents for the two or three things that only work as
> objects: something with a signature on it, something with a number in it,
> something someone did not mean for the player to see.*

So the collection **is** that list, scaled from one chapter to five.

### How it works

**Fifteen real surviving documents.** The player never picks one up. They are
a student in front of a primary source, so they do the only thing available:
copy it down. No magic, no paradox, no explanation required.

The notebook gains a second tab:

```
  WHAT I WAS TOLD          WHAT I COPIED DOWN   12/15
```

Four rules keep it a mechanic rather than a shelf:

1. **Documents are knowledge-gated, exactly like conversation topics.** You
   cannot ask Parris to see his contract until you have counted his woodpile
   and heard him complain about the village committee. Copying a document is
   the *reward* for the conversation work, never a parallel activity.
2. **Documents contradict people.** Ann Putnam Jr. tells you something in her
   own voice in March; her sworn deposition says it differently in June — and
   the handwriting is her father's. The sourcing skill becomes mechanical
   instead of explained.
3. **Every one is real and citable.** Archive reference on the reader screen,
   and they export into the exit artifact as citations. That is the DBQ move.
4. **The reader is a reader, not an organiser.** Original text on one side, a
   plain modern gloss on the other — a screen the design doc already
   requires. No fields, no prompts, no boxes to fill.

### The thing you genuinely cannot have

The real "you cannot act" moment is not about objects. It is about speech,
and it belongs in June, in the jail.

The topic list will contain:

> *"Tell her what happens on the nineteenth of July."*

Let the player choose it. Let them say it out loud.

**And she does not believe them.** No magic, no paradox, no rule about time
travel — she is a covenanted member of her church with thirty-nine
neighbours ready to sign for her, and a strange child has just said something
absurd. She is kind about it. She changes the subject and asks whether the
player has eaten.

That is the whole thesis of the game in one conversation, it costs a single
topic, and it requires no mechanism whatsoever.

## Interlude A — the parsonage cellar hole · present · ≈ 2 min

**Where.** The excavated foundation of the Salem Village parsonage, off
Centre Street in Danvers. A real site: a rectangular hole lined with
fieldstone, in a patch of trees behind a residential street, with an
interpretive sign and nothing else.

**Transition.** The player walks to the parsonage in March and steps through
— and is standing in the cellar hole of the same building. Locations rhyme.
The 1692 map and the present map share their geography.

**Who.** A fictional archaeologist, mid-dig. Speaks about objects, never
about meaning.

**The point.** *How poor, how cold, how close together did these people
live?* Everything they say is physical:

- The whole house would fit inside a modern classroom.
- Two rooms and a loft. Nine or ten people, including Tituba and John Indian.
- Redware sherds, a bone-handled knife, window lead, pins. Pins constantly.
- The hearth is enormous relative to the house, because it was the only heat.
- No privacy anywhere in the building. Not one room a child could be ill in
  without the whole household present.

**The line that does the work.** Asked what the objects prove, she declines
to say. *"They tell you the shape of the room. What people did in a room that
shape is your problem, not mine."*

**Goal:** *Look at what is left of the parsonage.*
**Documents:** none. This interlude is deliberately object-only.

## Chapter two — June 1692 · ≈ 8 min

**When, exactly.** Mid-to-late June. Bridget Bishop was hanged on 10 June.
The Court of Oyer and Terminer has sat once and will sit again on 30 June.
Rebecca Nurse is in the Salem jail awaiting trial. Tituba has been in jail
since her confession on 1 March and will never be tried.

So **the two people from March who are now in prison are both people the
player sat with** — Tituba in her kitchen, Rebecca Nurse by her fire. That is
not a coincidence to arrange; it is what actually happened, and it is why
March was built around those two.

**What has changed in the village.**

| March | June |
|---|---|
| Sleepy, muddy, empty road | Carts, strangers, people from Andover and Beverly |
| Tituba at the parsonage hearth | Gone. The hearth is cold and Parris does not want to discuss it |
| Rebecca Nurse gets up to greet you | House quiet. Francis Nurse is there. She is not |
| Ann Putnam Jr. mending, bored | Has filed formal complaints. Speaks with authority and is listened to by adults |
| Mercy Lewis hauling water | Afflicted. Now one of the accusers, and no longer a servant in the ordinary sense |
| Ingersoll wiping a clean bar | Full house, doing the best trade of his life, visibly unhappy about it |
| Parris aggrieved about firewood | Taking notes at examinations. Getting exactly the congregation he preached about |

**New this chapter.**

- **Mary Warren.** Afflicted, then recanted, then accused, then rejoined the
  afflicted. She demonstrates the machine's logic better than any argument:
  *the only exit from accusation is accusation.* She is the most frightening
  person in the game and she is about twenty.
- **The Salem jail.** A new location at the far end of the road.

**The goal, and why it reuses the road.**

> *Rebecca Nurse is not at home. Find out where she went.*

The answer is: five miles south, in the town the player already walked to.
The June chapter's destination is the jail, and getting there means walking
the road again — the same road, the same five miles, now in the other
emotional direction. The road is built. This costs nothing and pays enormously.

**The jail.** Small, dark, cramped. Historically the prisoners were charged
for their food and their irons, and Sarah Good's infant died there. The scene
is: you sit with a seventy-one-year-old woman who is certain this will be
sorted out.

She is not despairing. That is the point. She talks about the petition her
neighbours are signing, and about getting home for the harvest, and she asks
after people in the village. Then the topic appears and the player can warn
her, and she does not believe them.

**Contradiction work.** Everyone's account of March has shifted. What Ann
Putnam Jr. said about her mother in March, she now says differently. What
Ingersoll told you about the witch cake, he now hedges. The game never
adjudicates — but the player has notes from March, and the notebook will show
both.

**Documents:** 5, 6, 7, 8.
**Goals:** find out where Rebecca Nurse went · walk to Salem town jail · sit
with her · talk to the afflicted girls · read the petition her neighbours
signed.

## Interlude B — the trial papers · present · ≈ 2 min

**Where.** A reading room with the actual court records in it. The
Massachusetts trial papers survive and are held in Salem; the scene is a
fictional archive room, a fictional legal historian, and real documents on a
table.

**Transition.** The player leaves the meetinghouse in June — where the court
sat — and arrives in the room where its paperwork ended up.

**The one place a modern character is allowed to explain.** Everywhere else
in the present the rule is *ask, don't answer*. Here it is suspended, because
the material is genuinely technical and because this is the interlude that
does the heaviest lifting in the game:

- **Spectral evidence.** A witness testifies that the accused's *shape*
  tormented them. Nobody else can see it. There is no possible defence.
- **No counsel.** The accused had no lawyer. They cross-examined witnesses
  themselves, under the pressure of a courtroom full of screaming.
- **The lost charter.** Massachusetts had no legal government from 1689 to
  May 1692. This court was created by a brand-new governor, in a hurry,
  outside the usual structures.
- **Confession as survival.** Not one person who confessed was executed.
  Everyone hanged had refused to confess. The court's own logic rewarded lying
  and killed the honest.

**The reframe, stated once and plainly:** these were not people who did not
know any better. There were rules, the rules were followed, and the rules were
the problem. That sentence is the thesis of the whole interlude.

**The ergot red herring lives here.** The player will have heard it by now —
put it in the historian's mouth as a question a visitor always asks, and let
her take it apart: symptoms don't match, whole households ate the same bread,
and it explains nothing about *who* was accused. Ninety seconds, and the most
valuable ninety seconds in the game.

**Goal:** *Find out how the court could do this.*
**Documents:** none new — this is where documents already copied get
re-examined. The historian can pull up the player's own copies.

## Chapter three — September 1692 · ≈ 6 min

**When.** Late September. Giles Corey was pressed to death on 19 September.
Eight people were hanged on 22 September — the last executions. Nobody knows
yet that they were the last.

**The design of this chapter is silence.** No locked doors, no gates, no
barriers. Just people who spoke to you in March and now have nothing to say.

- The parsonage door opens. Parris is inside. He answers, briefly, and does
  not look up.
- The Nurse house is occupied and quiet. Francis Nurse is alive and will talk
  about the harvest and nothing else.
- Ingersoll's is empty in the middle of the day.
- Two houses on the map have nobody in them at all, and the game does not
  comment.
- The woman at the well who told you everything in March says good day and
  keeps walking.

**Content beats, all as testimony, none depicted:**

- **Giles Corey.** A neighbour tells you, flatly, over a fence. Two days
  under stones for refusing to plead — which kept his estate out of
  forfeiture, so his sons inherited. Follow the money.
- **The bodies.** The families of the hanged were denied burial in
  consecrated ground. Some went out at night to bring them home anyway. Told
  by someone who will not say who did it.
- **Mary Easty's petition.** She asks not for her own life. Document 9.
- **Ann Putnam Sr.** Grief that has found a use.

**The noticing.** Once, and only once, late in the chapter: Ann Putnam Jr.
looks directly at the player and speaks to them.

She has been twelve years old for six months. She has filed more complaints
than almost anyone in the county. And she asks the player something — not
who they are, but whether they have been here before, because she thinks she
has seen them.

The player cannot answer. There is no dialogue option. She waits, and then
goes back inside.

**Build exactly one of these.** A second makes it a mechanic instead of a
rupture.

**Documents:** 9, 10, 11, 12.
**Goals:** go back to the village · find out what happened to Rebecca Nurse ·
find out who is still willing to talk to you.

## Interlude C — the memorial, again · present · ≈ 3 min

**The loop closes.** Same map as chapter zero, same twenty benches, same
threshold stones. The player walks back in through the entrance they walked
in through at the start, and this time they know every name on the wall.

**Nora is still there.** She asks how it went. She is the only person in the
game who can ask that question.

**The descendant.** A woman with ancestors on both sides of it — one hanged,
one who signed a complaint. Thousands of living people are in exactly this
position; it is the ordinary condition of being from Essex County. She is not
tortured about it and she is not casual about it.

**The three closing documents.**

- **Ann Putnam Jr., 1706.** Standing in the village church while her apology
  is read aloud over her head by the minister. She is twenty-six. She never
  married, raised her siblings, and died at thirty-seven. The player met her
  at twelve. **This is the spine of the game closing.**
- **Sewall, 1697.** One judge stood up in Old South and took the blame in
  public. Stoughton, who admitted spectral evidence in the first place, never
  apologised and became governor. Put them side by side and say nothing.
- **Elizabeth Johnson Jr., 2022.** The last name cleared, 329 years late,
  because an eighth-grade civics class decided it was ridiculous. Nora
  already tells this story in chapter zero — here it lands differently.

**The ending.** The game asks, once:

> *What caused it?*

A free-text box. No word count, no rubric, no scoring, no right answer, and
no response from the game beyond acknowledging that it has been written down.

Then the export: their answer, every piece of evidence they gathered, who
each piece came from, and every document they copied, with citations. Plain
text, ready to paste.

**Goal:** *Say what you think.*
**Documents:** 13, 14, 15.

## Build order

Ordered so that each step produces something playable rather than a pile of
half-finished systems.

1. **The document mechanic**, with the four March documents. Tests the reader
   screen, the notebook's second tab, and the export — on content that
   already exists. Nothing new to write except four transcriptions.
2. **Interlude A** (parsonage dig). Small: one map, one character, no
   documents. Proves the chapter-transition pattern and the "locations rhyme"
   geography before it has to carry anything heavy.
3. **Chapter two: June.** The largest single piece of work. Re-dresses the
   village map, adds the jail, changes every existing character's dialogue,
   adds Mary Warren, and contains the warning scene.
4. **Interlude B** (trial papers). One room, one character, the ergot
   rebuttal, and a re-reader for documents already copied.
5. **Chapter three: September.** Re-dresses the village a third time. Mostly
   subtraction — the writing is short because the chapter is about silence —
   but the noticing has to be exactly right.
6. **Interlude C** and the ending. Closes the loop, three documents, the
   free-text answer, the full export.

### Scope, honestly

The engine is done. Everything above is content: maps re-dressed with props
that already exist, dialogue in a format that already runs, and documents in
a reader that needs building once.

The two things that are genuinely new code are the **document reader** (step
1) and the **free-text answer box** (step 6). Everything else is writing.

The largest risk is chapter two. It is the longest chapter, it re-writes six
existing characters, and it contains the scene the whole game is built to
deliver. It should not be rushed and it should be play-tested with an actual
student before September is written.

## Still open

- **The Wabanaki gap.** Carried forward from the design doc and still not
  closed. Mercy Lewis's thread is told entirely as settler trauma. This has
  to be resolved before chapter two ships, because June is where the frontier
  material does its real work. Published interviews or actual consultation —
  not a character written from scratch.
- **Does the player character have a name?** Chapter zero currently avoids
  the question. The September noticing may require an answer, or may be
  stronger without one.
- **Should June let the player visit Tituba too?** She is in the same jail.
  The scene is available and it is the natural place for the design doc's
  point that her confession under a beating supplied the imagery everyone
  else then borrowed. Cost: one more room and one more conversation. Probably
  worth it.
